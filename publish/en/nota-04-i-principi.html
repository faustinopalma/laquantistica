Clarification The principles: two formulations

In the introduction the principles are four, preceded by a principle zero; in this chapter they are five and written differently. These are not two theories: they are two choices, each suited to the purpose it serves.

The four principles in the introduction are the hypotheses of a derivation: they serve to obtain the Schrödinger equation, that is to describe how the state evolves between one measurement and the next. A derivation uses only the hypotheses it needs, and for that calculation there is no need to know the state in which a measurement leaves the system: this is why repeatability is stated beforehand, as principle zero, and not among the four.

In this chapter, by contrast, measurement is central and must be stated at once: the machines are cascaded, and to predict what the second will do we must know the state the first one left it in. This is the principle of measurement and eigenstates, that is principle zero read together with its consequence: the state the system is in after the measurement.

The other differences are explained in the same way. Conservation of the total is, among the possible formulations, the most economical one: from it and from linear superposition follows the conservation of scalar products, as we show in Note 02. Agreement with Newton, finally, only makes sense where there is motion in space to compare with classical mechanics: in this chapter there would be nothing to compare.

A remark on principle zero. In the literature, repeatable measurements are called of the first kind, and those that destroy the system of the second kind (the distinction goes back to Pauli). Repeatability holds for the former: stating it without qualification is, as textbooks warn, a simplification, because many real measurements consume what they measure. Stern-Gerlach apparatuses, however, are of the first kind — the atoms pass through and carry on — and this is precisely what allows them to be cascaded.
