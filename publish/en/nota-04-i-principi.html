Clarification The principles: two formulations

In the introduction the principles of Quantum Mechanics are four; in this chapter they are five and are written differently. These are not two theories, but the same theory stated at two levels, for two different purposes.

The formulation in the chapter is meant to let the theory emerge from the experiments: it speaks of quantities that are measured, of states told apart by a measurement, of a few discrete possibilities. It is the right language for Stern–Gerlach apparatuses, where the possible outcomes are two. The formulation in the introduction is instead the general one, written for a particle moving in space: there the possible values are the infinitely many positions, the vector of complex numbers becomes a function, and one can ask the question that leads to the Schrödinger equation.

Two entries deserve a comment. Conservation of the total is not a principle in the chapter: it is a property we derived, in the form of conservation of scalar products. In the introduction, by contrast, it is assumed, because it is needed to fix the form of the evolution equation. Agreement with Newton, conversely, would make no sense in the chapter: it only applies when there is motion in space to compare with classical mechanics, and it is the requirement that brings out the Schrödinger equation.

The passage from one formulation to the other is carried out in chapter six: there the principles are generalised to several quantities at once and then to the continuous case, where the vector of complex numbers becomes the function ψ. From that point on the principles hold in the form given in the introduction.
