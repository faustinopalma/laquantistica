Chapter 6

Further Developments of the Theory

In card four we introduced the Schrödinger equation for a charged particle in an electric potential. This equation allows us to determine ψ(x,y,z,t)\psi(x,y,z,t)ψ(x,y,z,t), that is the distribution of probability amplitudes as a function of time for the triple of measurable quantities (x,y,z)(x,y,z)(x,y,z). The central aim of the present card will be to determine the distribution of probability amplitudes for other observable quantities, in particular for momentum, angular momentum and energy.

To achieve this aim it is necessary to review and generalise the principles of Quantum Mechanics in the simplified version summarised below.

Principles of Quantum Mechanics.

For ease of reading we set them out in full: they are the three principles of card two, spelled out in the individual statements we shall work on, with the vectors understood to be normalised.

A physical system is characterised by its physical quantities, which can be measured.

When a quantity g is measured, several outcomes are possible, and each outcome has a certain probability of occurring. For every possible result g=g‾g=\overline{g}g=g​ there exists a state in which the measurement of g yields the value g=g‾g=\overline{g}g=g​ with certainty. Such a state is called an eigenstate of g with eigenvalue g‾\overline{g}g​.

Any other state α can be considered as a superposition of the eigenstates of a quantity g and can be represented by a vector of complex numbers ∣α⟩=(α1⋮αn)|\alpha\rangle=\left(\begin{gathered} \alpha_1 \\ \vdots \\ \alpha_n \end{gathered}\right)∣α⟩=​α1​⋮αn​​​. In the statement about measurement the very concept of eigenstate is introduced, that is a state such that if a measurement is performed a given value, called the eigenvalue, is obtained with certainty. In the case of a material particle the eigenstates, for example of the quantity x, are those represented by vectors of the form

Another example is given by the state represented by the vector

ψ(x,y,z)=δ(x−x‾)δ(y−y‾)δ(z−z‾)\psi(x,y,z)=\delta(x-\overline{x})\delta(y-\overline{y})\delta(z-\overline{z})ψ(x,y,z)=δ(x−x)δ(y−y​)δ(z−z)

The principles we have given are in fact a simplified version, because it is assumed that, given a value g‾\overline{g}g​, the eigenstate associated with g‾\overline{g}g​ is unique, which in general is not true; for example in the case of a material particle in three-dimensional space the eigenstates associated with an eigenvalue x‾\overline{x}x of x are infinite:

Now suppose we want to compute the distribution of probability amplitudes associated with physical quantities other than position, for example momentum p‾\overline{p}p​. (From now on, symbols with an overbar will denote vector quantities.)

Probability amplitudes for momentum.

If we know the state of a material particle, and we know the distribution of probability amplitudes for the position variable ψ(x,y,z)\psi(x,y,z)ψ(x,y,z), how can we determine the distribution of probability amplitudes for the quantity momentum?

If we knew the eigenstates of momentum then we could apply the fourth postulate, but we do not know these eigenstates and we must determine them.

To find the solution to our problem we must take the other end of the thread, that is we suppose we know what the eigenstates of momentum are, and from these we try to compute a value that we already know by another route, so that we can have a link between the things we know and those we have not yet determined.

Now we generalise the results obtained for momentum with the following very important theorem:

With every physical quantity ggg is associated a Hermitian operator GGG such that:

The mean value of the quantity ggg, when the system is in a state represented by the ket ∣ψ⟩|\psi\rangle∣ψ⟩, is given by the formula ⟨ψ∣G∣ψ⟩\langle\psi|G|\psi\rangle⟨ψ∣G∣ψ⟩;

Let us consider a quantity ggg, let g′g'g′ be the possible values of the quantity ggg, and let ∣g′,ξ⟩|g',\xi\rangle∣g′,ξ⟩ be the kets representing the eigenstates of the quantity ggg and of the set of auxiliary quantities ξ\xiξ; then the operator

G=∬g′ ξ∣g′,ξ⟩g′⟨g′,ξ∣ dξ dg′G=\iint_{g'\:\xi}|g',\xi\rangle g'\langle g',\xi|\:d\xi\:dg'G=∬g′ξ​∣g′,ξ⟩g′⟨g′,ξ∣dξdg′

is Hermitian and satisfies the two points of the statement. To verify it, one need only follow exactly the same steps that we followed for the operator PxP_xPx​.

Compatible quantities and incompatible quantities.

Suppose we want to perform a simultaneous measurement of xxx and of pxp_xpx​. After such a measurement the system should settle into a simultaneous eigenstate of the quantities xxx and pxp_xpx​, and the resulting state ket should be an eigenket of the operators XXX and PxP_xPx​, but this is impossible. Indeed, denoting by ∣x,px,ξ⟩|x,p_x,\xi\rangle∣x,px​,ξ⟩ a hypothetical eigenstate of the quantities xxx, pxp_xpx​ and of a suitable set of auxiliary quantities ξ\xiξ, we have

−iℏI∣x,px,ξ⟩=0⇔∣x,px,ξ⟩=0\begin{aligned} -i\hbar I|x,p_x,\xi\rangle & =0\Leftrightarrow \\ |x,p_x,\xi\rangle & =0 \end{aligned}−iℏI∣x,px​,ξ⟩∣x,px​,ξ⟩​=0⇔=0​

Energy.

As the last quantity of study we consider energy.

In the formalism of Classical Mechanics, for a charged particle in an electric potential, we have the energy
