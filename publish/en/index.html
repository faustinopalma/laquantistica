Introductory Course

Fundamental Experiments of Quantum Mechanics

The Schrödinger equation derived from four principles and one experimental measurement.

Introduction

A beam of electrons passes through a thin crystalline layer and produces a series of rings on a screen: a diffraction pattern, characteristic of waves. How can we explain this result if electrons are particles? The classical description of electron motion cannot explain this pattern. Interpreting it requires a different theory: Quantum Mechanics.

The course starts with electron experiments and diffraction. It then introduces the necessary mathematical tools and, from four principles taken as premises, builds an equation describing the evolution of a particle’s state. The resulting theory is applied to Rutherford scattering and the energy levels of hydrogen.

The experiments

The experiments with electrons, electron diffraction, Rutherford scattering, Franck–Hertz, the photoelectric effect and atomic emission spectra were carried out in the laboratory. The cards describe their apparatus and measurements.

For Stern–Gerlach, by contrast, the apparatus was designed but not built for cost reasons: the card analyses its theoretical operation. Cascaded Stern–Gerlach experiments are treated as thought experiments.

In this web edition, each experiment is accompanied by an interactive simulated laboratory, where you can vary the parameters and observe how the results change. The simulations accompany the descriptions of the experiments without replacing laboratory measurements.

The theory: four principles and one measurement

We derive the Schrödinger equation for a non-relativistic particle in a static electric potential, without taking it as a postulate. Instead, we take the following four principles as premises:

  1. Complex function. The state of a particle is described by a function that assigns a complex number to each position. The probabilities of finding the particle in the various regions of space are proportional to the sums, over each region, of the squared moduli of the function.
  2. Linear superposition. Two states can be combined, each with a complex coefficient, and the combination is still a state. The state evolves continuously in time and, under evolution, each term evolves as it would on its own, with the same coefficient, and the subsequent state is the sum of the evolved terms.
  3. Conservation of the total. As long as the system is not observed, the sum of the squared moduli over all positions does not change during the evolution.→ These three principles, together, are enough to fix the general form of the evolution equation. They do not yet say how the forces acting on the particle enter it.
  4. Agreement with Newton. Under the conditions in which Newton’s mechanics is confirmed by experience, the theory must give the same predictions (F = ma).→ We seek a Hamiltonian that satisfies this requirement and derive the Schrödinger equation, containing a constant whose value is not determined by the principles. We obtain its value from the diffraction of an electron beam through a thin crystalline layer: it coincides with Planck’s constant.

In cards 10 and 11 we return to the first three principles through the Stern–Gerlach experiment and the cascaded thought experiments, which illustrate their meaning without constituting a proof.

How to read this site

The treatment requires knowledge of Newtonian mechanics and electromagnetism, but not Analytical Mechanics. The course is intended for third-year Engineering students.

The work is divided into thirteen experimental and theoretical “cards”. The first two cover electrons and diffraction; the third brings together the background on complex numbers and state vectors needed for the derivation in cards 4 and 5. Reading in order is recommended, but the experimental cards (1, 2, 6, 8, 9, 10 and 13) can be read first and the theory afterwards.

The origin of the work and the web edition

The material originated in a 1999 degree thesis devoted to carrying out the experiments and analysing them for teaching purposes. The experiments were performed using LEYBOLD equipment, partly at the LAFIDIN teaching laboratory and partly in Milan, in the “demonstration hall” of LEYBOLD DIDACTIC. The Stern–Gerlach apparatus was also designed at the LAFIDIN laboratory.

The treatment of cascaded Stern–Gerlach experiments draws on R. P. Feynman (The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. III), using a different formalism and graphical representations intended to aid understanding.

The 1999 original had nine cards, some combining the experimental and theoretical parts, which are now separate. This edition changes their order and adds the card on complex numbers and state vectors. The reasons for these changes are described in the note What has changed since 1999.
